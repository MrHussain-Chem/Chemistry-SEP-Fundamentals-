Your separate chemistry plan

This plan takes you step by step from today to your two chemistry exams. It covers everything in GCSE Chemistry, including the extra separate chemistry topics. Each week has one clear job, a video lesson for each part of it, and everything else you need linked in one place. You will be learning most of this for the first time, so the plan teaches it step by step, and Mr Hussain marks the work you send him. If you used your first chemistry plan, you have already started on atoms and calculations, and this plan builds on them.

How to revise chemistry

The quickest way to remember chemistry is to test yourself on it. Pulling an answer out of your memory makes it stick far better than reading it again, and the harder it feels, the stronger the memory once you get it.

The five steps

  1. Find it. Find your weak topics from your marks, not from how you feel. My progress adds up the marks you have lost in each topic and shows which to work on first.
  2. Understand it. Watch the video lessons for the topic, pausing to make notes. Then make a one-page summary sheet in your own words, using the Learn links on the Checklist. If you can't put something in your own words yet, write it down as a question for Mr Hussain. If a video doesn't click, search YouTube for Freesciencelessons and the topic name to hear it explained another way.
  3. Learn it. Copy your sheet onto a whiteboard or paper, reading it out loud as you write, two or three times. Then cover everything and write it again from memory. Add what you missed in a different colour and go again. When you can write it from memory two or three times, you've learnt it for today. Four days later, do it again.
  4. Test it. Do the Try it questions and past-paper questions with your notes closed. Then change pen colour, add anything you missed, and mark it. If the answer was on your sheet but not in your head, go back to step 3. If it wasn't on your sheet, add it.
  5. Colour it again. Update the Checklist: has Not yet become Getting there, or Getting there become Got it? Then move on to the next topic.

On this page, Not yet, Getting there and Got it work like red, amber and green in the video.

One 30-minute session

  1. Set a timer for 30 minutes and open this week's topic on the Checklist. In your first session on a new topic, use the whole 30 minutes for the video lessons and your summary sheet.
5 min
Warm up from memory. With your notes closed, answer three questions: one from this week's topic, one from last week and one from last month. Then mark them. If you got one wrong, that's your brain telling you it needs that one again.
10 min
Understand one chunk. Watch one video lesson, or read one Learn box on the Fundamental Chemistry website or one section of the PMT notes. Close it, then add the key points to your summary sheet in your own words. Watching is only useful if you then do something with it.
5 min
Learn it. Cover your sheet and write that chunk from memory, then check it. Put any definition, test or colour on a flashcard.
10 min
Test it. Do one Try it question, including a calculation if the topic has one. Mark it, save your score, and add anything you missed to your sheet.

Make it stick

  1. Spread it out. Four or five 30-minute sessions on different days work much better than one long session.
  2. Flashcards. Making flashcards is not revising; testing yourself with them is. Say the answer before you turn the card. Only move a card to the back of the pack once you've got it right three days in a row. PMT has ready-made flashcards for every topic.
  3. A calculation every session. Calculations come up on both papers and the methods repeat every year, so practise one each time.
  4. Brain dump. Once a week, pick an earlier topic and give yourself 3 minutes to write down everything you can remember about it. Then check it against your summary sheet.
  5. Mix it up later on. From April, the final push weeks mix questions from different topics. It feels harder, and that is why it works.
  6. If writing is slow, say your answers out loud instead. And sleep well, because sleep helps your brain hold on to what you learnt.

On days you can't get going

Tell yourself you will do just five minutes, set a timer and start. Most of the time you will keep going, and if you don't, five minutes still counts. If 30 minutes feels too long at first, start with sessions of 10 to 15 minutes and build up. Put your phone in another room before you start.

Exam practice: two slow, one fast

The first time you do a set of exam questions on a topic, allow three minutes a mark. The next time, allow two minutes a mark. Then do it at exam speed, about one minute a mark. Speed comes from knowing the chemistry well, so going slowly at first is not wasted time. The timed papers on the Timeline follow this pattern.

  5. If the answer was in your notes and you had learnt it, but you didn't realise that was what the question wanted, write it down as a question for Mr Hussain. That is the kind of thing a lesson would normally sort out, and he can help with it.

What you need on the day

  • The paper comes with a periodic table. No equations are given, so learn every one in the Maths table in Exam favourites.
  • Bring a black pen and a spare, a pencil, a ruler, a rubber and a scientific calculator, in a clear pencil case. Water goes in a clear bottle with the label off. Phones and smartwatches are not allowed in the exam room.
  • Allow about one minute a mark: 100 marks in 1 hour 45 minutes. If a question is taking too long, move on and come back to it.
  • Write in black ink and use pencil only for graphs and diagrams. If you run out of space, use the extra pages at the back and write "continued on page …" next to the question.
  • Your exam centre will tell you where to go, when to arrive and your candidate number. If you have never sat an exam in an exam room before, ask Mr Hussain what to expect.

Six-mark answers

Six-mark questions are marked in levels. Each type has its own top-level description in the mark scheme, so plan your answer to match it. From 2018 to 2025, the six-markers that came up most were life cycle assessment (four times) and potable water (three times), then making salts, cells, explaining properties from structure and bonding, and ion tests (twice each).

Describe a method or plan
Top level: "The method would lead to the production of a valid outcome. The key steps are identified and logically sequenced."
Write numbered steps in the order you would do them. Name the equipment and amounts, say what you measure and how, and finish with repeating and how you use the results. Making salts, titrations, temperature changes, rates, chromatography and identifying ions all come up this way.
Explain
Top level: "Relevant points (reasons / causes) are identified, given in detail and logically linked to form a clear account."
Build chains with "because" and "so", for example: higher temperature, so particles have more energy, so more frequent successful collisions, so a higher rate.
Evaluate or justify
Top level: "A judgement, strongly linked and logically supported by a sufficient range of correct reasons, is given."
Give points for both options, use numbers from the table, link each point to an effect, then finish with a judgement that follows from your reasons. Life cycle assessments, choosing materials and choosing fertilisers come up this way.
Compare
Top level: "Scientifically relevant features are identified; the way(s) in which they are similar / different is made clear."
Write about both things in every sentence, using words like "both", "whereas" and "higher than", and include numbers when they are given.

The eight required practicals

Questions about the required practicals made up about 16% of the marks on the Higher papers from 2018 to 2025, and electrolysis, titration and identifying ions carried the most. Maths you will need

Calculations come up on both papers, and the same methods are used every year. Show every step, because marks are given for correct working even when an earlier number is wrong.

CalculationHow
Relative formula mass (Mr)Add up the relative atomic masses of every atom. Ca(OH)2 = 40 + (16 + 1) × 2 = 74
Relative atomic mass from isotopes(mass × % of each isotope, added together) ÷ 100. Lithium: (6 × 7.6 + 7 × 92.4) ÷ 100 = 6.9
Conservation of massMass of reactants = mass of products. If a gas escapes, the mass seems to go down
Concentration in g/dm³mass (g) ÷ volume (dm³). Change cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000
Uncertaintymean ± half the range, for example 16.5 ± 0.3 °C
Percentage yield CHEMISTRY ONLYmass of product made ÷ maximum theoretical mass × 100
Atom economy CHEMISTRY ONLYMr of useful product ÷ total Mr of all reactants × 100
Surface area to volume ratio CHEMISTRY ONLYFor a cube: (6 × side²) ÷ side³. When the side gets 10 times smaller, the ratio gets 10 times bigger
Rf valuedistance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent. No units, always less than 1
Mean ratequantity of reactant used or product formed ÷ time. Units such as g/s, cm³/s or mol/s
Moles HIGHER TIERmoles = mass ÷ Mr. One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles
Reacting masses HIGHER TIERmass to moles, use the equation ratio, then moles to mass
Concentration in mol/dm³ HIGHER TIERmoles = concentration × volume in dm³. Used in every titration calculation
Gas volumes HIGHER TIERvolume (dm³) = moles × 24 at room temperature and pressure
Bond energies HIGHER TIERenergy to break bonds minus energy released making bonds. A negative answer means exothermic
Gradient of a tangent HIGHER TIERDraw a tangent at the time asked, then change in y ÷ change in x
Balancing from masses HIGHER TIERChange each mass to moles, divide by the smallest, and use the whole-number ratio as the numbers in the equation
Percentage by mass of an element(Aᵣ × number of atoms of that element) ÷ Mᵣ × 100. Iron in Fe₂O₃: 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = 70%
MeanAdd up the repeats, leaving out anomalous results, and divide by how many you used
Significant figuresCount from the first digit that is not zero: 0.004 70 has 3 significant figures. Give the number asked for, and only round at the end
Standard form6.02 × 10²³ is 602 000 000 000 000 000 000 000. 0.000 25 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴. Use the ×10ˣ button on your calculator
RearrangingIf moles = mass ÷ Mᵣ, then mass = moles × Mᵣ and Mᵣ = mass ÷ moles. Check by putting numbers back in
Changing unitscm³ ÷ 1000 = dm³. kg × 1000 = g. 1 tonne = 1 000 000 g. 1 nm = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m
Plotting a graphIndependent variable on the x axis. Label both axes with units, choose scales that use at least half the grid, plot with small crosses in pencil, then draw a line or smooth curve of best fit that ignores anomalous points

Working scientifically

In a classroom you would pick up these words while doing experiments. They are tested on both papers, often in the practical questions, so learn the exact meanings. Watch: Accuracy, precision and error · Evaluating and improving a method · Reading graphs and drawing conclusions · Correlation and cause

WordMeaning
HypothesisAn idea that explains an observation and can be tested
Independent variableThe one you change
Dependent variableThe one you measure
Control variableOne you keep the same. Name it exactly, for example "the same volume of acid", not "the same amounts"
Categoric and continuousCategoric variables are names, such as the type of metal (plot a bar chart). Continuous variables are numbers, such as temperature (plot a line graph)
AccurateClose to the true value
PreciseRepeated readings are close to each other
RepeatableThe same person, using the same method and equipment, gets similar results
ReproducibleSomeone else, or a different method or equipment, gets similar results
ResolutionThe smallest change a measuring instrument can show
RangeFrom the smallest to the largest reading. For uncertainty, use half of (largest − smallest)
UncertaintyHow far the true value could be from your result: mean ± half the range
Random errorReadings scatter unpredictably above and below the true value. Repeating and taking a mean reduces its effect
Systematic errorEvery reading is out by the same amount in the same direction, for example a balance that reads 0.2 g with nothing on it (a zero error). Repeating does not fix it
Anomalous resultA result that doesn't fit the pattern. Leave it out of the mean and the line of best fit, and repeat that reading if you can
ValidThe method really tests what it is meant to, with only the independent variable changing
Hazard, risk and precautionA hazard could cause harm (acid is corrosive). The risk is the chance of harm. A precaution reduces the risk (wear eye protection)
CorrelationTwo variables change together. That alone does not prove one causes the other
Peer reviewOther scientists check work before it is published

Questions you will see: Why repeat and calculate a mean? (To reduce the effect of random errors and to spot anomalous results.) How could the method be improved? (Name one specific change and say why it helps, for example a lid on the cup reduces energy lost to the surroundings.) What can you conclude? (Describe the pattern, and quote numbers from the graph or table.)
